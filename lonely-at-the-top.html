Lonely at the Top: Why High Achievers End Up Without Close Friends

Half of CEOs report loneliness in the role, and most of them believe it is hurting their performance. Here is why success reliably strips out close friendship, what it costs, and what actually rebuilds it.

There is a specific kind of quiet that arrives after things go well. The revenue is up. The title landed. The season went the way you trained for. And somewhere in that same stretch you noticed that you have stopped telling anyone the truth about how you are.

It is easy to read that as a personal failing — you got busy, you let friendships slide, you should call people more. That framing is both common and wrong. What is happening to you is structural. It happens to nearly everyone who climbs, in every field, and it has been measured.

The short answer: success does not make you worse at friendship. It systematically removes the conditions friendship needs — symmetry, unguarded time, and people with nothing riding on your answer.

What the data actually says

This is not a soft observation. A landmark Harvard Business Review study found that half of CEOs report experiencing loneliness in the role, and of those, 61% believe it is hindering their performance. In 2024, 55% of CEOs reported mental health struggles including anxiety, depression and burnout — a sharp rise on the year before.

It is worse for people without an executive team around them. Solo founders and small-business owners report higher rates than CEOs of larger organisations, and among founders under 34, roughly 31% name emotional loneliness as a significant struggle. With close to 30 million solopreneurs now in the US economy, social isolation has quietly become the default operating condition for a large share of ambitious people.

Organisational research also makes the cost concrete: chronic loneliness measurably reduces task performance, narrows creativity, and degrades reasoning and decision quality. Which means for anyone whose job is mostly judgment, this is not a wellbeing footnote. It is a performance variable.

The four mechanisms that do the damage

Ask a hundred high achievers how they ended up with no one to call and you will get four stories, usually overlapping.

1

Asymmetry replaces reciprocity

Friendship runs on mutual exposure. But once you are the one who signs the cheques, sets the strategy or holds the room together, the exchange stops being even. People bring you their problems; you cannot bring them yours. Over a few years, every relationship in your life quietly becomes one-directional.

2

Everyone acquires a stake

Your co-founder has equity. Your team has job security. Your spouse has a mortgage attached to your decisions. Your industry peers have a competitive interest. None of these people are being dishonest — they simply cannot give you a disinterested answer, and you know it, so you stop asking.

3

Time gets optimised into nothing

Close friendship is built in inefficient time — the drive, the long dinner, the second beer where the real thing finally comes out. High performers are very good at eliminating inefficient time. The unstructured hours where intimacy used to happen are the first thing the calendar eats.

4

You get too good at sounding fine

Composure under pressure is a professional asset. You have trained it for years. The problem is that it does not switch off at 6pm, and eventually the people closest to you are responding to a version of you that you are performing rather than living. Then, when you do want to say something true, you find you have lost the muscle.

The people who could tell you the truth are gone, and the people who are left have a reason to tell you something else.

Why “just make more friends” does not work here

The standard advice fails for a specific reason: it treats this as a volume problem when it is a match problem. You almost certainly know hundreds of people. You are probably in several groups. The issue is that none of them contain someone who is simultaneously (a) carrying a comparable weight, (b) not affected by your answer, and (c) in a setting where the real version is the expected version.

That combination almost never occurs by accident once you are successful. It has to be built deliberately.

A network

  • Optimised for opportunity
  • Everyone is presenting
  • Reciprocity is transactional
  • Being candid carries a cost

A room

  • Optimised for honesty
  • Presenting is the thing you drop at the door
  • Reciprocity is emotional, not commercial
  • Being candid is the price of admission

What actually rebuilds it

Three things, in this order.

Peers of comparable weight. Not mentors, not friends from before, not your team. People whose day contains the same category of pressure as yours. The recognition is the medicine — it removes the exhausting preamble where you have to justify why this is hard.

A setting with a floor under it. Unstructured “let’s catch up” will not do it, because you will both default to the performance. A facilitated session with a format and an agreement about confidentiality gives people permission to skip to the real thing. That is the entire function of a well-run group.

A method, not just a vent. Being heard is necessary and insufficient. Most high achievers do not want catharsis; they want the pattern to change. That means bringing an actual tool to the thing that is in the way — performance coaching, hypnotherapy, NLP or visualization, depending on what is holding the pattern in place. This is how those methods get used.

An honest boundary. Everything above is about coaching for people who are functioning and want to function better. If what you are carrying is grief, trauma, a clinical condition, or thoughts of harming yourself, that is a job for a licensed therapist — and it is a better job for them than for any group. In the US you can reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline any time by calling or texting 988.

Common questions

Why do successful people have fewer close friends?

Because success removes the conditions close friendship requires. It introduces asymmetry (people bring you their problems, not the reverse), gives nearly everyone around you a stake in your decisions, eliminates the unstructured time where intimacy forms, and rewards a composure that eventually becomes hard to drop. It is structural rather than personal.

Is loneliness at the top actually a performance issue?

Yes. Harvard Business Review research found 61% of the CEOs who report loneliness believe it is hindering their performance, and organisational studies link chronic loneliness to reduced task performance, narrowed creativity and impaired reasoning — which is most of what senior judgment consists of.

What is the difference between a network and the kind of room that helps?

A network is optimised for opportunity, so everyone in it is presenting and candour has a cost. A room is optimised for honesty, so dropping the performance is the price of entry rather than a risk. You can have an enormous network and still have nowhere to say the true thing.

Do I need therapy or coaching for this?

If you are functioning, performing, and privately running on empty, coaching with peers is usually the right fit. If you are in crisis, working through trauma or grief, or need diagnosis or treatment, that is therapy — and no group session substitutes for it.

How do group sessions for high achievers work?

Five or six people carrying comparable weight meet for ninety minutes with a facilitator. The format is a brief honest check-in, an extended focus on whoever needs it most that week, one tool applied to the pattern in the room, and one concrete thing each person commits to before the next session.
